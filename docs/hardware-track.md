## Ignite Next x Intel — Stop Simulating, Start Moving: See It. Detect It. Act on It. The Intel Physical AI Challenge

### Summary
AI is great on screen, but have you ever made it move? Welcome to the Intel Physical AI Challenge at Junction. This weekend, we’re taking your models out of the cloud and giving them hands, eyes, and real-time brains

### Description
**Introduction**

In many factories, people still inspect parts by eye and pull out the faulty ones, because programming a robot for the job takes specialist effort. With physical AI, you show a robot a task a few dozen times and it learns a policy it can run on its own. Ignite Next and Intel bring this to Junction, so you can take a real robot arm from first demonstration to an autonomous inspection cell in one weekend. You train your models on cloud GPUs and run them on Intel Core Ultra Series 3 (Panther Lake) laptops with Intel’s open-source robotics stack, which is built to take robot AI from the lab into production.

**The Challenge**

Each team gets its own station with an SO-101 robot arm, a leader arm for teleoperation, a top-view camera, an inspection camera and a box of Duplo bricks, some of which you mark as defective yourself. Start by teleoperating the arm to record demonstrations, then fine-tune a vision-language-action (VLA) policy in Physical AI Studio on cloud GPUs. In parallel, train an Anomalib model that tells good bricks from defective ones at the inspection point. Then bring both together: the arm picks each brick, shows it to the inspection camera and drops it into the “good” or “defective” bowl, fully autonomously, with your models optimized with OpenVINO and running on the laptop’s CPU, GPU and NPU. Teams that get there early can push further with faster cycles, messier brick piles, new defect types or their own twist on the task.

## Aalto University — Aaltonomous Humanoid

### Summary
Our humanoid robot can do backflips but can't sort a few blocks into boxes. Give it a brain.

### Description
**Introduction**

Humanoid robot hardware has improved enormously in recent years. Robots can now do impressive things like backflips, dancing and kung fu. They are also becoming surprisingly affordable: the Unitree G1 costs around €50,000, and it won't be long before humanoids are within reach of ordinary people. But there's a catch, known in robotics as Moravec's paradox. Hard-coded backflips are relatively easy for a robot, while something as simple as picking up a small block and putting it in the right box is much harder, even though a small child can do it without thinking. Our G1, Väiski, is physically very capable, but it's still pretty dumb: it has very little autonomy or onboard intelligence. During this hackathon, your job is to give it a brain.

**The task**

Make the G1 sort objects into boxes on its own, without a human controlling it. Väiski stands behind a counter with its legs and waist held stiff, working with two 7-DoF arms and two Dex3 three-fingered hands. In front of it, 3 to 5 printed objects lie at random positions and angles: cubes, cylinders and triangular prisms, about 4.5 cm in size, in red, yellow and blue. Behind the objects are three identical orange boxes (21 cm wide) at fixed positions: left, centre and right, in a row, 5 cm apart from each other and 40 cm from the robot's torso. The boxes have no markings; they are told apart only by their position. The robot has to put every object into the right box within 2 minutes.

- Fixed sorting: red objects go into the left box, yellow into the centre box, blue into the right box.
- Sort by instruction: a text instruction defines which box each object goes into, e.g. "Sort the objects: red into right, blue into left, yellow into center." Expect phrasings you haven't seen before.
- Stretch goal: put each object through the matching hole in a lid on one box.

Your policy sees the head camera (colour + depth), both wrist cameras, the joint states and the instruction, and sends back target positions for all arm and finger joints, 30 times a second.

**How it works**

- Simulation first: build and test your solution in a ready-made Isaac Sim simulation of the robot and our exact scene, with plenty of GPU power from Verda.
- Real-world data during the event: you don't need any data to get started. During the hackathon, the Aalto University team will publish datasets recorded with the real robot at the venue on Hugging Face, with links shared in the Junction Discord. You can use them for training and for tuning your solution to the real cameras and conditions.
- Submit a Docker image: your solution runs as a small policy server in a container. Our runner sends its commands to the simulator or the robot through our safety filter.
- Finals: we evaluate submissions in simulation, on a version of the scene you can't train on, and pick the finalists. They run on the real robot on Sunday. The best real-robot score wins.

- Winning Team: 2,000€ + a visit to the Aalto robotics lab
- Top 5: 🤖 **Top ~5 teams** (chosen by performance in simulation) get to run their solution **live on the real Unitree G1**.
